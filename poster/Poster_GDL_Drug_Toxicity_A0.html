Graph & Geometric Deep Learning
for Drug Toxicity Prediction

A scaffold-split benchmark of fingerprints, ChemBERTa, 2D GNNs, 3D SchNet, and multitask learning across five toxicity tasks.
Konstantina Paschou   Georgios Kesoglidis   Theocharis Angelidis Supervisor  Prof. Grigorios Tsoumakas
Core finding
Scaffold splits reveal when graph learning actually generalizes.

GNNs help on scarce clinical tasks. Fingerprints stay strong on larger datasets. LD50 gains disappear under scaffold splits.

Full Benchmark8 methods, 5 datasets, one scaffold split protocol
Beyond the headline gap
ChemBERTa is weakest on every classification task Frozen language-model features do not transfer to unseen scaffolds.
GIN and GINE lead the graph models Bond features in GINE help most on the structure-sensitive endpoints.
Random Forest still wins the large datasets Graph models lead only on the scarce, imbalanced clinical tasks.
4A
LD50 Split Leakage

Random splits reward near-neighbour memorization. Scaffold splits test extrapolation to unseen chemical cores.

Every GNN's LD50 R² falls below zero under scaffold splits.
4B
3D Geometry Check

3D geometry is not automatically better. Approximate conformers add spatial signal, but also noise.

SchNet does not beat the 2D bond-aware GINE here.
4C
Selective Tox21 Transfer

Sharing one GIN backbone across all 12 endpoints lowers macro AUROC. Only related endpoints benefit.

Multitask learning helps only for related endpoints.

Conclusions
1
Use the task regime first. Scarce clinical endpoints suit GIN.
2
Trust scaffold splits. LD50 random-split gains do not hold.
3
Use 3D carefully. SchNet trails GINE but adds an uncertainty signal.
4
Share tasks selectively. Tox21 transfer is not uniformly helpful.

Setup & Reproducibility

RDKit featurisation, PyTorch Geometric, scikit-learn and XGBoost. All runs use a shared Bemis-Murcko scaffold splitter for cross-method comparability. Per-run weights and metrics are checkpointed for reproducibility.
